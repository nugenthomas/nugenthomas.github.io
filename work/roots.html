Roots: A farm-to-table concept

Product Design
2017

A 2017 solo concept exploring how farm-to-table groceries could feel as easy as a typical grocery app, without losing the transparency and freshness that make local food worth buying.

Farmer's markets in New York City are small, scattered, and hard to get to. People who want to support local farms still end up at the chain supermarket because the alternative takes too much time.

Roots is a 2017 solo concept. I owned the research, brand, and consumer-side mobile flows. The goal was to make farm-to-table groceries feel as easy as a typical grocery app, without losing the transparency and freshness that make local food worth buying.

Research as the design brief

I ran a survey and follow-up interviews, then built three personas: a busy young professional, a working dad, and a retired food enthusiast. I synthesized the responses into a one-page brief that named what Roots had to deliver: transparent information about each farm, next-day freshness, a lower carbon footprint than the supermarket, value for cost-conscious urbanites, and editorial content that kept users coming back. Every section below maps to one of these.

Onboarding that explains the supply chain

Most grocery apps onboard with a zipcode and a list of products. Roots needed users to understand the supply chain first, because the supply chain is the product. The first four screens walk through it: place an order, the verified farm harvests it, Roots staff pack it at the farm, and it ships to the user's door or a nearby pickup market. The final screen captures a zipcode and checks whether the user is inside the 100-mile sourcing radius.

Browsing built around deals and farm transparency

The brief told me to optimize the browse screen for two things: value and transparency. Deals lead, so cost-conscious users see savings first. Every listing card links to a farm detail page where users can read the farm's story and see the rest of its produce. The quick-add button on each card keeps the experience fast for users who already know what they want.

A photo strategy that shows real produce

Supermarket photos are uniform: identical tomatoes, identical apples, all sized and colored to a single standard. Roots' photos lean the other way. Each photo shows the exact amount in one order, captured to highlight heirloom varieties, irregular shapes, and the variation that comes from actual farming.

Shopping lists for planners

Some users plan a week of meals at a time, others plan a party or a holiday. Shopping lists supports both. Users build, name, and reuse lists like "Favorites" or "Gina's birthday party." Each list shows a running subtotal, and the full list can be added to the cart in a single tap.

Checkout with two pickup paths and a live order timeline

Checkout shows the full cost breakdown and how much more the user needs to add for free shipping. Users pick between home delivery and market pickup, with the pickup screen showing nearby market locations along with their hours and dates. The order confirmation screen carries a live timeline (Ordered → Harvested → Delivered) and gives users a short window to change the pickup location or add forgotten items.

Editorial content that shops itself

The Roots Journal publishes recipes and food-culture features. Each recipe lists its ingredients with a single "Add all" button, so a reader can buy the whole recipe in one tap or add individual products from the cards below. The Journal pulls in users who read for inspiration and converts them into shoppers on the same screen.

Looking back

Roots was a concept, so it never shipped. The discipline that came out of it, turning research findings into a fixed brief that every design decision had to justify itself against, is the part I still use on every project today.
